# Are We Building Technology Faster Than We Can Govern It?

The honest answer is yes.

But the honest answer is also incomplete — because the question assumes that governance and technology are running the same race, on the same track, toward the same finish line.

They are not.

---

## The Standard Narrative

The standard narrative goes like this: technology moves fast, regulation moves slow, and the lag in between is where bad things happen. Fix the lag, fix the problem.

It's a clean story. It's also wrong in the ways that matter.

The problem is not velocity. It's **architecture**.

Governance systems — legislatures, courts, international bodies, regulatory agencies — are built for a world in which power is legible. A factory you can inspect. A chemical you can test. A product with a fixed set of capabilities that don't change overnight.

None of those assumptions hold for software. Especially not for AI.

A large language model can be updated silently. Its capabilities can expand between one audit and the next. Its behavior varies by prompt. It operates at scale no human regulator can personally observe. And its most consequential applications — credit scoring, medical triage, criminal risk assessment, content moderation — run behind interfaces that obscure the model entirely.

You cannot regulate what you cannot see. And the infrastructure of modern technology is, almost by design, hard to see.

---

## A Structural Problem, Not a Speed Problem

Consider what happened with social media.

Platforms like Facebook and Twitter weren't unregulated because lawmakers were slow. They were unregulated because no one had built the conceptual vocabulary to describe what they were doing. Were they publishers? Utilities? Town squares? Private clubs?

The answer mattered — because each category carries a different liability regime, a different set of obligations, a different theory of harm.

While that debate ran in circles for a decade, algorithmic amplification rewired political epistemology in dozens of countries. The mechanisms were documented by researchers. The harms were measurable. The governance architecture simply had no hook to hang a response on.

That is not a speed problem. It is a **category problem** — the absence of legal and institutional frameworks capable of even naming the thing they are supposed to govern.

The same dynamic is now playing out with AI. Faster, with higher stakes.

---

## The Three Gaps That Actually Matter

When people talk about technology outpacing governance, they usually collapse three distinct problems into one.

**Gap one: the comprehension gap.**

Legislators and judges are generalists operating on technical systems that require years of domain expertise to understand at depth. This isn't a failure of intelligence — it's a structural feature of representative governance. You cannot elect a semiconductor engineer to every committee that might eventually touch a chip.

The result: governance bodies that cannot independently evaluate the claims vendors make about their own products. They rely on expert witnesses, industry lobbying, and the occasional Stanford professor. The informational asymmetry is not incidental to the governance failure — it is the governance failure.

**Gap two: the jurisdiction gap.**

The infrastructure of modern technology is global by default. A model trained in San Francisco, hosted on servers in Dublin, accessed by a user in Jakarta, making decisions that affect people in Lagos — which country's laws apply?

The EU's AI Act, the UK's AI Safety Institute, the US Executive Orders, China's generative AI regulations — these are all national or supranational responses to systems that operate at civilizational scale. Coordination between these frameworks is partial, contested, and slow. The technology does not wait for treaty negotiations.

**Gap three: the accountability gap.**

Who is responsible when an AI system causes harm?

The developer who trained the model? The company that deployed it? The platform that integrated it? The user who prompted it? In a world of foundation models, APIs, and fine-tuned wrappers, the chain from capability to consequence is long, diffuse, and structurally designed to be deniable.

The semiconductor in your computer cannot be sued. The developer who wrote the code that exploited a vulnerability usually can't be either. The company that shipped the product with the vulnerability often faces no criminal liability. We have spent fifty years building a legal architecture that systematically exteriorizes the costs of software failure.

We are now deploying AI into consequential domains — healthcare, finance, criminal justice, autonomous weapons — using the same liability architecture we built for spreadsheet applications.

## What "Move Fast and Break Things" Actually Broke

The Silicon Valley growth ethos was not naive. It was rational, given the incentive structures it operated within.

If you can capture market share before competitors, externalize costs onto users and society, and face no liability for the downstream effects of your product choices — then moving fast and breaking things is the optimal strategy.

Facebook moved fast. It broke democratic institutions in Myanmar, the Philippines, and the United States. Mark Zuckerberg testified before Congress. The stock price recovered.

Uber moved fast. It broke taxi licensing frameworks, labor law, and insurance structures in every city it entered. It paid fines. It changed a few policies. It went public.

The lesson the industry absorbed was not "be more careful." It was "move fast enough that the cleanup cost is someone else's problem."

This is not a character flaw in tech founders. It is the entirely predictable output of an incentive structure that decouples the benefits of innovation from the costs of its failure modes.

AI is that same logic applied to systems with greater capability, greater opacity, and greater potential for irreversible harm.

## The Governance Responses, Evaluated Honestly

**The EU AI Act** is the most comprehensive legislative response to date. It creates a risk-tiered framework — high-risk applications face strict conformity requirements, real-time oversight obligations, and data governance rules. It names specific prohibited uses: social scoring, real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces, AI that manipulates psychological vulnerabilities.

Its limitations are significant. It passed after years of negotiation that softened its sharpest edges. Its enforcement depends on national competent authorities with uneven capacity. And it applies to systems deployed in the EU — not to the training pipelines that produce them, which sit largely in the US and China.

**The US approach** remains fragmented. Executive orders from the Biden administration established voluntary commitments from frontier AI labs, required safety testing disclosures, and directed federal agencies to develop sector-specific guidance. The Trump administration rolled back most of that. What remains is a patchwork of sector-specific rules — HIPAA for healthcare AI, fair lending requirements for credit AI, FTC oversight of deceptive AI claims — without any coherent framework underneath.

Voluntary commitments are not governance. They are the offer of governance in exchange for the absence of governance.

**International coordination** is nascent. The Bletchley Declaration was a start. The Seoul and Paris AI Safety Summits built on it. The Hiroshima AI Process produced a set of principles. None of these have enforcement mechanisms. They are statements of intent by governments that are simultaneously racing each other for AI capability advantage.

The contradiction at the heart of international AI governance is that the countries most capable of shaping it are also the countries most unwilling to constrain their own development.

## The Private Governance Problem

In the absence of effective public governance, private companies have become de facto regulators of some of the most consequential AI decisions.

Anthropic publishes a model card and a Constitutional AI framework. OpenAI maintains a usage policy. Google has AI Principles. Meta has a Responsible AI framework.

These documents are not nothing. They reflect genuine internal deliberation about the tradeoffs involved in deploying capable AI systems.

But they are also unilateral, unaccountable, and revocable.

A company can update its usage policy on a Tuesday afternoon. It can train a new model with different behavioral properties and describe it as an improvement. It can decline to disclose what its safety evaluations revealed. It faces no external audit requirement, no mandatory incident reporting, and no personal liability for the people making these decisions.

The Pentagon-Anthropic dispute earlier this year illustrated exactly where private governance breaks down. Anthropic held a line on autonomous weapons and domestic surveillance. The Pentagon threatened its contract, its security clearances, and its legal standing. The company held — barely, and at enormous cost — and the outcome was not guaranteed.

Private governance is only as durable as the company's ability to absorb the cost of holding the line. That is not a governance architecture. It is a bet on the virtue and financial resilience of specific private actors.

---

## The Capability Frontier Moves Regardless

Here is the sharpest version of the problem:

The AI capabilities that will define the next decade are, in many respects, already determined. The training runs are either complete or in progress. The models being deployed today were designed two to three years ago. The safety research that might inform better governance of future systems is running on parallel tracks with limited cross-pollination.

We are governing the AI of 2023 with frameworks designed for the AI of 2020, while the AI of 2027 is already being trained.

Regulatory lag is not a temporary condition. It is a chronic feature of governing exponentially improving systems with linearly improving institutions.

This does not mean governance is futile. It means the theory of governance must change.

The goal cannot be to catch up to the current frontier. It must be to build infrastructure — legal, technical, and institutional — that is capable of constraining a frontier that does not yet exist.

That requires funding technical expertise inside regulatory bodies. It requires mandatory transparency about training data, evaluation results, and incident logs. It requires international coordination with enforcement teeth, not just principles. It requires liability structures that reconnect the costs of AI failure to the entities making deployment decisions.

None of that is impossible. None of it is even especially controversial in the abstract.

It is merely extremely difficult to achieve inside political systems that are themselves being reshaped by the technology they are trying to govern.

---

## The Honest Frame

The question — are we building technology faster than we can govern it? — has a structural answer: yes, because we built the technology to move faster than governance by design.

The more useful question is: **what would it take to change that design?**

Not slower AI development. Not a moratorium on frontier research. Not the naive hope that technologists will self-regulate their way to safety.

What it would take is treating governance infrastructure as a capability investment, not a compliance cost. It would take governments that are willing to enforce rules on their own most strategically valuable companies. It would take an honest accounting of the fact that the countries leading on AI capability are also the countries most exposed to AI's failure modes.

None of this is inevitable. But none of it happens without acknowledging the structural nature of the problem.

Saying "regulation is slow, technology is fast" is a description of a symptom.

The disease is that we built systems of incentive, liability, and accountability that make the gap not just likely but rational.

---

_The gap between what AI can do and what we have decided it should do is, at present, mostly filled by the preferences of a small number of private companies. That is not a stable equilibrium._
